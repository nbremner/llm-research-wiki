# Institutional Readiness and Transformational Barriers: Artificial Intelligence Adoption Frameworks for Organizational Delivery Capability

**Citation.** Razdan, V. (2026). *Institutional Readiness and Transformational Barriers: Artificial Intelligence Adoption Frameworks for Organizational Delivery Capability*. Research Square. https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-8696847/v1

**Summary.** This Research Square preprint proposes a conceptual AI-adoption readiness framework for project-management contexts spanning technical infrastructure, data governance, organizational capability, and ethical governance. It contrasts general-purpose foundation models with bespoke autonomous systems and recommends phased, decision-support-first implementation and capability building rather than treating adoption as a purely technical deployment.

## Key claims
- The paper argues that AI adoption in project management depends jointly on data maturity, technical integration, financial viability, organizational skills and culture, and governance rather than on model capability alone.
- It proposes domain-specific foundation models, phased use-case implementation, and ecosystem partnerships as transitional pathways for organizations that cannot support full bespoke AI systems.
- It frames project managers' evolving role as combining contextual judgment, stakeholder leadership, AI-output validation, and ethical oversight.

## Evidence & limitations
- The author describes a literature review (72 included documents), an assessment of 15 vendor offerings, and expert feedback from five project-management professionals and three AI implementation specialists; the paper does not provide enough detail to reproduce the review, vendor analysis, or expert-review results.
- The source is a January 2026 Research Square preprint, not peer-reviewed; Crossref records it as posted content under DOI 10.21203/rs.3.rs-8696847/v1 and the PDF states a CC BY 4.0 license.
- Several quantitative figures and application examples are not transparently supported by the cited references, which include references from unrelated health and cybersecurity domains; treat the framework as a low-confidence conceptual prompt rather than evidence for effect sizes, prevalence estimates, or causal implementation claims.
